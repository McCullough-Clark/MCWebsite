Every January I get a version of the same call. The books are "mostly done," the tax return is due, and somewhere between the bank statements and QuickBooks there is a number nobody can explain. Sometimes it is a clearing account that drifted. Sometimes it is a contractor who was paid all year and never sent a W-9. The work to fix it in January is always more than the work to prevent it in November, so this is the list we run for our own clients before the year closes, with the 2026 tax changes that actually change what a small business has to do.

I am an Enrolled Agent, and the figures below are from the IRS's 2026 inflation adjustments and the law passed in July 2025, usually called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. They apply to the 2026 tax year, the return you will file in 2027. Your CPA or preparer makes the final call on your return. The bookkeeping is what makes their job possible, and that part is ours.

## The tax changes that reach the bookkeeping

Most of what passed in 2025 is about rates and individual deductions, and it does not change how you keep books. A few pieces do.

The 1099 threshold went up. For payments made in 2026, you issue a 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC to a contractor or vendor when you paid them $2,000 or more during the year, not $600. That is the first real change to that number in decades, and it means fewer forms in January. It does not mean fewer W-9s. You still need a W-9 on file for anyone who might cross the line, and the rule is still that the income is taxable to the contractor whether or not a form is filed. The 1099-K that payment processors send also moved back to $20,000 and 200 transactions, so some owners who got a 1099-K from Square or PayPal for 2024 will not get one for 2026. The sales still have to be in the books. The form was never what made them income.

Bonus depreciation is back at 100 percent, and this time it does not expire. Equipment, furniture, computers, and most other property with a life of 20 years or less, bought and placed in service after January 19, 2025, can be written off in full in the year you start using it. Section 179 still exists alongside it, with a 2026 limit of $2,560,000 and a phase-out that starts at $4,090,000 in purchases, which is to say it is not the constraint for most of the businesses we work with. The constraint is the date. "Placed in service" means in use, not ordered and not sitting in a box. If a purchase matters for 2026, it needs to be running before December 31, and the invoice and the date need to be in the file.

The deduction for qualified business income is permanent now. It was scheduled to end after 2025, and a lot of planning advice from the last few years assumed it would. For 2026 the full 20 percent is available until taxable income passes $201,750 for a single filer or $403,500 on a joint return, and above that it phases down depending on the kind of business. The bookkeeping consequence is simple: the deduction is computed from your business profit, so the profit has to be right, and it has to be separated cleanly by entity if you have more than one.

Tips and overtime got their own deduction for employees, and that lands on the employer as a reporting job. Starting with the 2026 W-2, qualified tips go in Box 12 with code TP, qualified overtime goes in Box 12 with code TT, and tipped employees get an occupation code in Box 14b. Only the premium portion of overtime that federal law requires counts, so for an employee paid $15 an hour and $22.50 for overtime, the reportable amount is the $7.50, not the $22.50. Mandatory service charges are not tips. Nothing changes about withholding; this is reporting only. If you run payroll through a provider, the question to ask them before December is whether they are tracking the premium portion separately. If you run it yourself, you are the one tracking it.

A few individual figures are worth knowing because owners ask. The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for a joint return. The state and local tax cap is $40,400 for most filers, phasing down once income passes $505,000. The federal estate exclusion is $15 million. None of these touch the books. They touch the conversation you will have with your preparer in March.

## The checklist

This is the list, in the order we work it. Everything below assumes QuickBooks Online, but the steps are the same in any system.

- Reconcile every bank and credit card account through December 31, and look at every uncleared item older than 60 days.
- Reconcile the payment processor. Square, Jane, Stripe, Shopify, Toast: sales recorded gross, fees recorded as fees, payouts tied to deposits.
- Bring every clearing account to zero, or to a balance you can name line by line.
- Review accounts receivable. Write off what is uncollectible. Chase what is not.
- Review accounts payable and record bills received in December even if they are paid in January.
- Confirm every loan balance against the lender's year-end statement, with interest and principal split correctly.
- Pull the fixed asset purchases for the year and attach the invoice and the placed-in-service date to each one.
- List every vendor paid $2,000 or more and confirm a W-9 is on file. Request the missing ones now.
- Reconcile sales tax collected to what was filed.
- Count inventory on December 31 if you carry it.
- Run December payroll, including any bonuses, inside December, and confirm the W-2 tip and overtime coding with your provider.
- Record owner draws, distributions, and contributions, and make sure personal expenses did not land in the business.
- Pay the fourth-quarter estimate by January 15.
- Decide on retirement contributions. SEP and employer 401(k) contributions can wait until the return is filed, but employee deferrals into a 401(k) have to be elected by December 31.
- Close the period and set the closing date in QuickBooks so January entries cannot land in December.

## The items that cause the most trouble

The processor reconciliation is where most of the January calls come from, so I will spend a minute on it. A Square or Jane deposit is not a sale. It is a batch of sales, net of fees, arriving two business days late. If the deposit was categorized as income, revenue is low by the fees, the fee expense does not exist, and the last few days of December are sitting in January. Some of our clients run tens of thousands of dollars a month through a processor, and the fee line alone is a real number on the return. We wrote about this for [Square](/blog/square-quickbooks-sync-problems/) and for [Jane](/blog/jane-app-quickbooks-integration/) separately. The year-end version of the fix is to pull the processor's own report for the year, compare gross sales, fees, and payouts to what QuickBooks shows, and resolve the difference before the books close rather than after.

Clearing accounts are next. Undeposited Funds, a payroll clearing account, a Zelle or Venmo clearing account: each of these should be zero at year end, and when it is not, the balance should be a short list of specific items waiting for something. A clearing account with a balance that has been "about the same" since March is a balance nobody has looked at since March. I had one practice where months of Zelle deposits were parked in clearing because two patients had the same first name. The fix took an afternoon. Finding it took longer than it should have because the balance looked stable.

Then the W-9s. The new $2,000 threshold means fewer vendors cross the line, but the ones who do are the same ones who never answered the first request. A list of vendors over the threshold, pulled in November, gives you six weeks to collect the forms instead of the two weeks you get if you pull it in January. We send the request with the December payment, which is the last point where the vendor has a reason to reply quickly.

Fixed assets round out the list. The 100 percent write-off is only available if the asset was placed in service by December 31, and only provable if the invoice says what was bought and the file says when it went into use. A new treatment table, a point-of-sale terminal, a laptop, a vehicle: each one needs that date. If you are planning a purchase for the tax benefit, the question is not whether you can order it by December 31. It is whether it will be in use.
